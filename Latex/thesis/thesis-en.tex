\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

Deep learning is used in many everyday technologies, especially in systems that analyse images and video \cite{LeCun2015DeepLearning}. The models behind these systems are improving quickly, but they are also becoming larger and more demanding to run. While powerful servers can handle such models, many real-world applications require the model to run directly on a small device, such as those in cameras, robots, drones or mobile systems \cite{Howard2017MobileNets}. These devices have limited computing power, memory and battery capacity, so a model that performs well on a workstation may be too slow, too large or too energy-intensive to be practical on the device \cite{Sze2017EfficientProcessing}.

For this reason, there is a growing need to make models lighter and faster while keeping their results as reliable as possible \cite{Cheng2017SurveyCompression}. In practice, this means balancing several factors: the model's accuracy, its prediction speed, the memory it requires, and the energy it consumes. The best balance depends on the intended use: a safety-critical system may prioritise accuracy, while a battery-powered device may prioritise speed and energy efficiency.

To address these challenges, researchers develop methods that reduce the computational and memory requirements of a model while preserving as much of its accuracy as possible. Some approaches simplify the model structure, others reduce the numerical precision of the computations, and still others transfer knowledge from a larger model to a smaller one \cite{Han2016DeepCompression, Hinton2015Distilling, Jacob2018QuantizationIntegerOnly}. Practical performance also depends on the hardware and software used to run the model, so solutions have to be evaluated under realistic deployment conditions \cite{Cai2019ProxylessNAS}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{edge_devices.png}
    \caption{Variants of edge devices (reproduced from \cite{Abid2022EdgeDevices}).}
    \label{fig:edge_devices}
\end{figure}

None of these methods is without trade-offs. Each approach compromises some accuracy, training effort, or engineering complexity in favour of computational efficiency, and in scenarios where maximum accuracy is essential, a compressed model may offer no tangible advantage.
Furthermore, in safety-critical applications such as medical or automotive systems, a compressed model must undergo the same strict validation as its baseline counterpart.


\section{Compress the model, or choose differently?}
\label{sec:intro_problem}

Practitioners seeking a faster object detector have alternatives to compression. The simplest
approach is to select a more compact variant within the same model family, since modern detector
families are released in multiple sizes, as are the YOLO models used
throughout this thesis \cite{UltralyticsYOLO11,UltralyticsYOLO26}. Another option is to change
how the model is executed: its numerical precision, input resolution and aspect ratio. Standard numerical formats include 32-bit floating point (FP32), 16-bit brain floating point (BF16), and 8-bit integers (INT8). On a given CPU, however, reduced precision
accelerates inference only when it is supported by dedicated hardware instructions, such as Vector Neural Network Instructions (VNNI) for 8-bit integer arithmetic or Advanced Matrix Extensions (AMX) for BF16 and INT8 matrix operations in modern Intel server processors. Figure~\ref{fig:intro_routes} summarises these three pathways toward a more computationally efficient detector evaluated in this thesis. 


Compression methods, however, are typically evaluated against their parent models, demonstrating improved inference speed at the cost of a minor drop in accuracy. This approach does not address whether a smaller dense variant from the same model family could achieve equivalent speed and accuracy. An analysis of 81 pruning studies revealed a lack of standardised benchmarks and metrics, to a point where pruning methods are difficult to compare with one another \cite{Blalock2020StatePruning}. Another investigation found that for the structured pruning methods examined, training the resulting pruned architecture from scratch yielded comparable or superior accuracy relative to fine-tuning the pruned weights, prompting the authors to advocate for more rigorous baselines \cite{Liu2019RethinkingPruning}. Finally, computational savings are frequently reported in terms of reduced parameter counts or floating-point operations rather than actual latency on target hardware, even though these metrics can diverge significantly \cite{Sze2017EfficientProcessing}.


This thesis therefore treats right-sizing (choosing a smaller dense model) and deployment settings (number format, input size and input shape) as the baselines that any compression method has to beat. All of them are measured on the same processor and with the same accuracy protocol.

\section{Application: detecting grape bunches from a drone}
\label{sec:intro_application}

The research questions are initially investigated on COCO, the standard benchmark for object detection \cite{Lin2014COCO}, and subsequently evaluated within a precision viticulture application. In the mission examined here, which is part of the DRAGONS project, a drone equipped with a camera flies along vineyard rows to locate grape bunches, then approaches a bunch to collect a sample for ripeness assessment. The detector executes on board, so it must operate within the constraints of a compact compute module, while accommodating two vastly different spatial scales: distant detection during the search phase, and close-range detection during the approach phase. 

W{\l}odarczyk et al.~\cite{Wlodarczyk2026GrapesReady} catalogued publicly available vineyard datasets and assessed their suitability for 3D scene reconstruction. Their catalogue was used here to identify datasets annotated for grape bunch detection, resulting in the selection of two benchmarks for this thesis. GrapeMOTS \cite{ArizaSentis2024GrapeMOTS} comprises aerial drone footage captured along vineyard rows, where grape bunches appear small and hard to distinguish, representing the search phase. Conversely, the Embrapa Wine Grape Instance Segmentation Dataset (WGISD) \cite{Santos2020WGISD} consists of a series of close-range photographs of grape bunches, representing the approach phase. Both datasets are relatively small compared to COCO; for instance, WGISD contains only 300 images. This study therefore also investigates whether insights from large generic benchmarks are applicable to the smaller datasets typical of agricultural applications.


\section{Aim and research questions}
\label{sec:intro_aim}

The objective of this thesis is to determine under what conditions compressing an object detector yields a superior accuracy--latency trade-off on a given processor, compared to simpler alternatives of choosing smaller models, selecting a number format that matches the processor, or adjusting input resolution and aspect ratio. The study is guided by three research questions:


\begin{enumerate}
    \item[\textbf{RQ1}] \textbf{Generic data.} On a given processor, does structured pruning combined with knowledge distillation or quantisation-aware training ever achieve a more favourable accuracy--latency trade-off than simply choosing a dense model of the right size?
    \item[\textbf{RQ2}] \textbf{Deployment settings.} To what extent do the numerical precision, the instruction set of the processor and the input size and shape affect the accuracy and latency of object detection models, compared with compression techniques?
    \item[\textbf{RQ3}] \textbf{Transfer to an application.} Do the findings of RQ1 and RQ2 hold for the long-range (search) and close-range (approach) views of the vineyard datasets, and which configurations best suit each scenario?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope}
\label{sec:intro_scope}

The study is deliberately constrained, so that all comparisons are conducted under identical conditions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Models.} The nano and small variants of two convolutional detector families, YOLO11 and YOLO26 \cite{UltralyticsYOLO11, UltralyticsYOLO26}, alongside YOLO26m serving as a larger reference and a teacher model in selected experiments. A transformer-based detector, D-FINE \cite{Peng2024DFINE}, is also included as a limited cross-family check on the vineyard datasets.
    \item \textbf{Methods.} Structured channel pruning, knowledge distillation, post-training quantisation and quantisation-aware training, evaluated alongside the deployment configurations mentioned above.
    \item \textbf{Hardware and runtime.} Inference is benchmarked on an Intel Xeon 6520P server processor restricted to four cores, using the OpenVINO toolkit \cite{OpenVINO}. While this processor natively supports AMX, edge-class hardware is approximated by restricting the execution to AVX2 with VNNI, which is verified on the compiled execution graph. GPUs are used only for training.
    \item \textbf{Data.} COCO 2017, using a training subset for adaptation and the 5,000-image validation split for evaluation, together with the vineyard datasets GrapeMOTS and WGISD.
    \item \textbf{Exclusions.} Because physical embedded hardware was unavailable, latency is not measured on a real edge device.
\end{itemize}

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:intro_contributions}

The thesis makes the following contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{An evaluation protocol for compression aimed at CPU inference.} A compressed model is considered effective only if it surpasses both the linear interpolation between the dense nano and small variants of its own family, and the global Pareto frontier of all dense models at its measured latency. The criterion is paired with frozen model shortlists, pre-registered test-set evaluations, paired bootstrap confidence intervals, and an instruction-set-limited proxy for edge processors.
    \item \textbf{A negative result on generic datasets.} On COCO, no pruned and distilled or quantisation-aware model reached the dense line. Across all comparisons, compressed variants fell below the reference line by 1.0 to 3.9 AP. 
    \item \textbf{A conditional positive result on small agricultural data.} On WGISD, distillation improves the dense small model by about two mAP50-95 points. A 20\% pruned small model trained via distillation matches the accuracy of the distilled dense variant, while achieving nano-level latency with a rectangular $480\times640$ input. Control experiments show that distillation rather than pruning or extended training is the source of the improvement.
    \item \textbf{Empirical guidance on deployment settings.} Numerical precision must align with hardware capabilities (BF16 where AMX is available, INT8 on VNNI-class processors), while input resolution and aspect ratio govern the trade-off between accuracy and latency at least as strongly as the evaluated pruning ratios. 
    \item \textbf{A data audit for the vineyard mission.} This thesis evaluates the suitability of public datasets for the mission and diagnoses the failure modes of long-range detection on GrapeMOTS. Performance is limited by object size, lighting conditions and domain gaps, rather than model size.
    \item \textbf{Reproducible experiments.} Every reported number is traceable to hash-verified models, runtimes and result receipts.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Neural networks and their training}
\label{sec:rw_nn}

Neural networks are built from many simple computational units, often called \emph{neurons}, that transform their inputs into an output and can be connected into larger structures. An early mathematical model of a neuron was the threshold logic unit of McCulloch and Pitts \cite{McCullochPitts1943}, which later led to trainable models such as the perceptron \cite{Rosenblatt1958Perceptron}. As computing power and training methods improved, neural networks developed into specialised families: recurrent networks for sequences \cite{Hochreiter1997LSTM}, convolutional networks for images \cite{LeCun1998GradientBased}, generative adversarial networks for data generation \cite{Goodfellow2014GAN}, and transformers, which now dominate many results in language and vision \cite{Vaswani2017Attention}.

Training a network means finding parameter values that minimise an objective function \cite{LeCun2015DeepLearning}. For a model $f(\mathbf{x};\theta)$ with parameters $\theta$, a dataset $\mathcal{D}=\{(\mathbf{x}_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$ and a loss function $\ell$, supervised learning solves
\[
\min_{\theta}\; \mathcal{L}(\theta) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \ell\big(f(\mathbf{x}_i;\theta), y_i\big),
\]
often with a regularisation term such as $\lambda \|\theta\|_2^2$. The gradient $\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}$ is computed by backpropagation, which applies the chain rule through the computational graph \cite{Rumelhart1986Backprop}, and the parameters are updated by gradient descent, $\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta \nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(\theta_t)$, with learning rate $\eta$. Because the full gradient is expensive, training uses \emph{stochastic gradient descent} (SGD), which estimates it on a mini-batch $\mathcal{B}_t \subset \mathcal{D}$ \cite{RobbinsMonro1951}:
\[
\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta \nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}_t}(\theta_t),
\qquad
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}_t}(\theta)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}_t|}\sum_{(\mathbf{x},y)\in\mathcal{B}_t}\ell\big(f(\mathbf{x};\theta),y\big).
\]
Momentum accumulates a running direction of descent and smooths the noisy updates \cite{Polyak1964}:
\[
\mathbf{v}_{t+1} = \mu \mathbf{v}_t + \nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}_t}(\theta_t),
\qquad
\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta \mathbf{v}_{t+1},
\]
where $\mu \in [0,1)$ controls the influence of past gradients. Figure~\ref{fig:sgd} illustrates the effect.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{SGD_with_momentum.png}
    \caption{Stochastic gradient descent with and without momentum (reproduced from \cite{miller2025hmmMomentum}).}
    \label{fig:sgd}
\end{figure}

In practice, the optimiser, the learning-rate schedule (warm-up, step or cosine decay) and the regularisation (weight decay, data augmentation) are chosen together and strongly affect the result \cite{LeCun2015DeepLearning}. Training is a non-convex problem: many parameter settings reach a low training loss but generalise differently. A held-out validation set is therefore used to choose hyperparameters and the checkpoint to keep, and early stopping guards against overfitting. This matters most on small datasets, where long training can fit the training images closely while accuracy on new images falls.

A \emph{deep} network stacks many such layers, each a learned transformation followed by a non-linear activation. In vision, depth produces hierarchical features: early layers respond to edges and textures, deeper layers to object parts and whole objects \cite{LeCun2015DeepLearning}. Depth and width also determine the cost of inference, which depends on the number of parameters (storage), the number of arithmetic operations and the memory needed for intermediate activations. Memory traffic can be the bottleneck, so a reduction in parameters or operations does not always give a proportional speed-up \cite{Sze2017EfficientProcessing}.

\section{Convolutional neural networks}
\label{sec:rw_cnn}

Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are designed for data on a grid, most notably images \cite{LeCun2015DeepLearning}. They rely on \emph{local connectivity} and \emph{weight sharing}: a convolutional layer applies the same small kernel at every position of the image instead of learning a separate weight for every input value. This reduces the number of parameters and makes the layer \emph{translation equivariant}: a shifted object produces a correspondingly shifted response. Figure~\ref{fig:CNN} shows a typical architecture.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{Typical_cnn.png}
    \caption{CNN architecture visualisation. Reproduced from Aphex34 \cite{Aphex34_2015_TypicalCNN} (CC BY-SA 4.0).}
    \label{fig:CNN}
\end{figure}

For an input feature map $X \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times C_{\text{in}}}$ and a $k \times k$ kernel, the output at position $(u,v)$ and output channel $j$ is
\begin{equation}
Y_{u,v,j} = b_j + \sum_{c=1}^{C_{\text{in}}}\sum_{p=0}^{k-1}\sum_{q=0}^{k-1}
W_{p,q,c,j}\,X_{u+p,\,v+q,\,c},
\label{eq:conv2d}
\end{equation}
where $W$ are the kernel weights and $b_j$ is a bias. A stride larger than one or a pooling operation reduces the spatial resolution, and a non-linear activation such as $\mathrm{ReLU}(x)=\max(0,x)$ follows each convolution. Modern CNNs combine these operations into building blocks: residual connections that add skip paths and allow deeper networks, batch normalisation that stabilises training, bottlenecks that use $1\times1$ convolutions to reduce and restore the number of channels, and depthwise separable convolutions that factorise a convolution into cheaper parts \cite{LeCun2015DeepLearning, Howard2017MobileNets}.

The usual proxy for the cost of a convolutional layer is the number of multiply--accumulate operations (MACs) \cite{Sze2017EfficientProcessing}:
\[
\text{MACs} \approx H'W' \cdot C_{\text{out}} \cdot k^2 C_{\text{in}},
\]
where $H' \times W'$ is the output resolution. Two consequences matter for this thesis. First, because $H'$ and $W'$ scale with the input image, the cost of a convolutional network grows roughly with the number of input pixels, so the input resolution and aspect ratio are direct levers on latency. Second, the cost is proportional to the channel counts $C_{\text{in}}$ and $C_{\text{out}}$, which is what structured pruning reduces (Section~\ref{sec:rw_pruning}).

\section{Transformers}
\label{sec:rw_transformers}

Transformers were introduced for sequence modelling and replace recurrence with attention, which relates every element of the input to every other one \cite{Vaswani2017Attention}. For $n$ tokens with embeddings $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$, learned projections give queries $Q = XW_Q$, keys $K = XW_K$ and values $V = XW_V$, and scaled dot-product attention computes
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Attention}(Q,K,V)=\mathrm{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V.
\label{eq:scaled_dot_product_attention}
\end{equation}
\emph{Multi-head attention} runs several attention operations in parallel with different projections and concatenates their outputs, $\mathrm{MHA}(X)=\mathrm{Concat}(\mathrm{head}_1,\dots,\mathrm{head}_h)W_O$ (Figure~\ref{fig:multi-head}). A transformer block combines multi-head attention with a position-wise feed-forward network, residual connections and layer normalisation.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{Multi-Head-Attention.png}
    \caption{Multi-head attention (reproduced from \cite{Vaswani2017Attention}).}
    \label{fig:multi-head}
\end{figure}

The product $QK^T$ costs $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$ operations, so attention becomes expensive for long sequences \cite{Vaswani2017Attention}. Vision Transformers (ViTs) apply transformer encoders to images by splitting an image into $n = HW/P^2$ patches of $P \times P$ pixels, embedding each patch as a token and adding positional embeddings \cite{Dosovitskiy2020ViT}. For high-resolution inputs the number of tokens grows quickly, which motivated hierarchical designs with local windowed attention such as the Swin Transformer \cite{Liu2021Swin}, and methods that merge or drop redundant tokens during inference \cite{Bolya2023ToMe}. Token reduction is not evaluated in this thesis. Transformers enter it through the decoder of the detection transformers described in Section~\ref{sec:rw_detr}.

\section{Object detection}
\label{sec:rw_detection}

\subsection{The detection task}
\label{sec:rw_detection_task}

An object detector predicts, for every object in an image, a bounding box, a class label and a confidence score. \emph{Two-stage} detectors such as Faster R-CNN first propose candidate regions and then classify and refine each one \cite{Ren2015FasterRCNN}. \emph{One-stage} detectors predict boxes and classes directly from dense feature maps in a single pass, which makes them simpler and faster \cite{Redmon2016YOLO}. Dense prediction produces many overlapping candidates for the same object, so most one-stage detectors end with non-maximum suppression (NMS): among boxes that overlap by more than an intersection-over-union (IoU) threshold, only the highest-scoring one is kept. NMS is a sequential, data-dependent step whose cost grows with the number of candidates.

Detection accuracy is measured by average precision (AP), the area under the precision--recall curve, computed for each class and averaged over classes. The COCO benchmark, with 80 object categories, averages AP over ten IoU thresholds from 0.50 to 0.95 (mAP50-95) and also reports AP at IoU 0.50 (mAP50) \cite{Lin2014COCO}. The exact protocol used in this thesis is defined in Chapter~3.

\subsection{The YOLO family}
\label{sec:rw_yolo}

YOLO introduced the one-stage formulation in which a single network predicts boxes and classes over a grid in one forward pass \cite{Redmon2016YOLO}. Its successors improved the backbone, the feature aggregation in the neck, the assignment of training targets, the loss functions and the export tooling. The Ultralytics line used in this thesis is anchor-free and has a decoupled head, with separate branches for classification and box regression \cite{UltralyticsYOLOv8, Sapkota2025YOLOEvolution}. Each generation is released in several scales, from nano (n) through small (s) and medium (m) to large and extra large, obtained by scaling the depth and width of the same design and trained with the same recipe \cite{UltralyticsYOLO11, Jocher2026YOLO26}.

\textbf{YOLO11} builds its backbone and neck from C3k2 blocks and adds a block with spatial self-attention (C2PSA) after the backbone \cite{UltralyticsYOLO11, Sapkota2025YOLOEvolution}. Its head regresses each box side as a discrete probability distribution over a fixed range of bins, trained with the Distribution Focal Loss (DFL), and its predictions are filtered by NMS.

\textbf{YOLO26} keeps the general structure but simplifies the head for deployment \cite{Jocher2026YOLO26}. It removes DFL and regresses box sides directly, which gives a lighter head with an unconstrained regression range. It also uses a dual-head design. A one-to-many head assigns several predictions to each object during training and is filtered with NMS at inference; a one-to-one head is trained so that each object receives a single prediction, which allows inference without NMS. The documentation describes the one-to-many path with NMS as the default for accuracy and the NMS-free path as an option \cite{UltralyticsYOLO26}. Training adds three changes: MuSGD, a hybrid of SGD and the Muon optimiser; Progressive Loss, which shifts supervision towards the head used at inference; and STAL, a label assignment that guarantees positive training targets for small objects. Across the five scales, YOLO26 reports 40.9 to 57.5 mAP50-95 on COCO at 1.7 to 11.8\,ms of TensorRT latency on an NVIDIA T4 GPU \cite{Jocher2026YOLO26}.

Because YOLO11 and YOLO26 share one training framework and one export path, comparisons between them reflect the architecture generation rather than the tooling. The nano and small scales are the ones relevant for edge processors.

\subsection{Detection transformers}
\label{sec:rw_detr}

DETR formulates detection as direct set prediction \cite{Carion2020DETR}. A transformer encoder--decoder receives image features and a fixed set of learned object queries, and a bipartite matching loss assigns each ground-truth object to exactly one query. The model therefore needs neither anchors nor NMS. Its high computational cost, however, limited its use in real-time settings \cite{Zhao2024RTDETR}.

RT-DETR made the approach real-time with two changes \cite{Zhao2024RTDETR}. An efficient hybrid encoder separates interaction within each feature scale from fusion across scales, and an uncertainty-minimal query selection provides better initial queries to the decoder. The number of decoder layers can be reduced at inference to trade accuracy for speed without retraining. On a T4 GPU, RT-DETR outperformed contemporary YOLO detectors in both speed and accuracy.

D-FINE redefines box regression in DETR models \cite{Peng2024DFINE}. Fine-grained Distribution Refinement replaces the prediction of fixed coordinates by iterative refinement of probability distributions over box edges. Global Optimal Localisation Self-Distillation transfers localisation knowledge from the refined distributions to shallower layers. D-FINE is released in scales from nano to extra large.

RF-DETR uses weight-sharing neural architecture search to find accuracy--latency trade-offs of a detection transformer for a given target dataset, evaluating thousands of configurations without retraining \cite{Robinson2025RFDETR}. Its nano model reports 48.0 AP on COCO, 5.3 AP above D-FINE nano at similar latency.

All latencies reported in these papers are measured on GPUs with optimised runtimes. How detection transformers compare with convolutional detectors on processors, at equal accuracy, is rarely reported. This thesis includes D-FINE as a limited cross-family check for that reason.

\section{Making detectors cheaper}
\label{sec:rw_efficiency}

Deploying a detector on a device with limited computing power, memory and energy requires reducing the cost of inference while keeping accuracy as close as possible to that of an unconstrained model \cite{Cheng2017SurveyCompression, Sze2017EfficientProcessing}. This viewpoint is sometimes called \emph{frugal artificial intelligence}. The main families of methods reduce the numerical precision (quantisation), remove redundant structures (pruning), transfer knowledge from a larger model to a smaller one (knowledge distillation), or search for architectures that meet a hardware budget \cite{Gholami2021SurveyQuantization, Han2016DeepCompression, Hinton2015Distilling, Cai2019ProxylessNAS}.

\subsection{Quantisation and number formats}
\label{sec:rw_quantisation}

Networks are usually trained and stored in 32-bit floating point (FP32). Quantisation represents weights and activations with fewer bits, which reduces storage and memory traffic and, on hardware with suitable instructions, speeds up computation \cite{Gholami2021SurveyQuantization, Sze2017EfficientProcessing}. Figure~\ref{fig:number_formats} compares the formats relevant here. The 16-bit IEEE format (FP16) keeps more mantissa bits but has a much smaller range than FP32. The brain floating-point format (BF16) keeps the 8-bit exponent of FP32 and therefore its range, so conversion is simple and models usually run in BF16 without recalibration, at the cost of a coarser mantissa \cite{Kalamkar2019BF16}. INT8 represents values as 8-bit integers together with a scale.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{number_formats.pdf}
    \caption{Bit layout and range of the number formats discussed in this thesis. Floating-point formats store a sign, an exponent and a mantissa; INT8 stores an integer that is mapped to a real value by a scale (and a zero-point).}
    \label{fig:number_formats}
\end{figure}

In integer quantisation, a real-valued tensor $x$ is mapped to an integer tensor $x_q$ by an affine transformation with a \emph{scale} $s$ and a \emph{zero-point} $z$ \cite{Jacob2018QuantizationIntegerOnly}:
\begin{equation}
x_q = \mathrm{clip}\!\left(\left\lfloor \frac{x}{s} \right\rceil + z,\; q_{\min},\; q_{\max}\right),
\label{eq:quant_affine}
\end{equation}
where $\lfloor \cdot \rceil$ denotes rounding and $[q_{\min}, q_{\max}]$ is the integer range, for example $[-128,127]$ for signed INT8. The value is approximately recovered by
\begin{equation}
\hat{x} = s(x_q - z).
\label{eq:dequant}
\end{equation}
The parameters are derived from the observed range $[x_{\min},x_{\max}]$ of the tensor, for example $s = (x_{\max}-x_{\min})/(q_{\max}-q_{\min})$. They can be shared by a whole tensor (per-tensor) or defined separately for each output channel (per-channel), which usually preserves accuracy better for convolution weights. Symmetric quantisation fixes $z=0$ and is common for weights; asymmetric quantisation allows $z \neq 0$ and suits activations whose distribution is not centred at zero \cite{Gholami2021SurveyQuantization}.

\emph{Post-training quantisation} (PTQ) converts a trained model without retraining. A small calibration set is passed through the network to estimate the ranges of the activations, which depend on the input data and are harder to quantise than the weights. \emph{Quantisation-aware training} (QAT) simulates quantisation during training: tensors are quantised and dequantised in the forward pass, while gradients flow through the rounding with a straight-through estimator. QAT usually recovers more accuracy than PTQ at the same bit width, at the cost of extra training \cite{Gholami2021SurveyQuantization, Jacob2018QuantizationIntegerOnly}. The Neural Network Compression Framework (NNCF) implements quantisation and other compression methods for models deployed with OpenVINO \cite{Kozlov2020NNCF, OpenVINO}.

Object detectors are often more sensitive to quantisation than classifiers, because box regression depends on small numerical differences, and some operations, such as box decoding, are usually kept in higher precision. A quantised detector is therefore a mixed-precision graph rather than an all-integer one. Whether quantisation pays off depends on the hardware: if the runtime has no fast integer kernel for an operation, the operation falls back to floating point and the expected gain shrinks \cite{Sze2017EfficientProcessing}. Section~\ref{sec:rw_cpu} returns to this point for processors.

\subsection{Pruning}
\label{sec:rw_pruning}

Pruning removes parameters that contribute little to the output \cite{Cheng2017SurveyCompression}. With a binary mask $\mathbf{m}$ over the parameters, the pruned model uses $\theta' = \mathbf{m} \odot \theta$ and is usually fine-tuned afterwards to recover accuracy \cite{Han2016DeepCompression}. \emph{Unstructured} pruning removes individual weights, for example those with the smallest magnitude. It can reach high sparsity, but the remaining weights form irregular patterns that standard dense kernels cannot exploit, so the latency often does not decrease without specialised sparse hardware or software \cite{Sze2017EfficientProcessing}. \emph{Structured} pruning removes whole filters, channels, attention heads or blocks. The result is a smaller dense network that runs with ordinary kernels, and by the MAC formula of Section~\ref{sec:rw_cnn} removing output channels of one layer and the matching input channels of the next reduces the cost of both.

Pruning methods differ in how they rank structures. Magnitude criteria remove the filters with the smallest $\ell_1$ or $\ell_2$ norm \cite{Li2017PruningFilters}. Used on their own, they do not indicate how much to remove from each layer, which Li et al.\ determined with a per-layer sensitivity analysis. Taylor criteria estimate the change of the loss when a structure is removed from the product of its weights and their gradients \cite{Molchanov2019Taylor}. For a group $\mathcal{G}$ of parameters, the first-order estimate is
\[
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{G}} = \Big(\sum_{s \in \mathcal{G}} g_s\, w_s\Big)^2,
\qquad g_s = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_s}.
\]
Because it is computed from the training loss on real data, it can be compared across layers without a separate sensitivity analysis for each layer \cite{Molchanov2019Taylor}.

In modern architectures, channels are coupled: a residual addition, a concatenation or a split requires the same channels to be removed from several layers at once, otherwise the shapes no longer match. DepGraph models these dependencies automatically and groups the coupled parameters, so that any architecture can be pruned consistently \cite{Fang2023DepGraph}. Pruning can be applied in one shot, followed by fine-tuning, or gradually over several rounds of pruning and fine-tuning, which usually preserves accuracy better at the same final size \cite{Han2016DeepCompression}. In detectors, the backbone and neck carry most of the computation and are the usual targets, while the final prediction layers and attention blocks are often left intact.

The value of pruning has been questioned. For the structured pruning methods they examined, Liu et al.\ found that training the pruned architecture from scratch gave comparable or better accuracy than fine-tuning the inherited weights; they concluded that the pruned \emph{architecture} matters more than the inherited weights and called for stronger baselines \cite{Liu2019RethinkingPruning}. A meta-analysis of 81 papers found that the lack of standardised benchmarks and metrics makes pruning methods difficult to compare with one another \cite{Blalock2020StatePruning}. In addition, savings are usually reported in parameters or operations, and fewer operations do not translate one-to-one into lower latency (Section~\ref{sec:rw_cpu}). A pruned model is rarely compared with the obvious alternative: a smaller dense model of the same family, measured on the same device.

\subsection{Knowledge distillation}
\label{sec:rw_kd}

Knowledge distillation trains a compact \emph{student} model with the help of a larger or more accurate \emph{teacher} \cite{Hinton2015Distilling}. In addition to the ground-truth labels, the student learns from the teacher's outputs, which carry information about similarities between classes and about the teacher's uncertainty. In the classic formulation for classification, teacher and student logits $\mathbf{z}_T$ and $\mathbf{z}_S$ are softened with a temperature $\tau$,
\begin{equation}
p_T(\tau) = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{z}_T}{\tau}\right),
\qquad
p_S(\tau) = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{z}_S}{\tau}\right),
\label{eq:kd_softmax_temp}
\end{equation}
and the student minimises
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = (1-\lambda)\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{sup}}(y, p_S(1))
+ \lambda\,\tau^2\,\mathrm{KL}\!\big(p_T(\tau)\,\|\,p_S(\tau)\big),
\label{eq:kd_loss}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda$ balances the two terms and the factor $\tau^2$ keeps the gradient magnitudes comparable across temperatures \cite{Hinton2015Distilling}. Detectors such as YOLO use an independent sigmoid per class instead of a softmax, and the distillation term then becomes a binary cross-entropy between the teacher's and the student's per-class probabilities. Distillation can also act on intermediate features, attention maps or relations between samples.

Distillation for detection must transfer both classification and localisation. Early methods mostly imitated deep features in selected regions. Localisation distillation showed that mimicking the teacher's outputs can outperform feature imitation and that localisation knowledge is more important than semantic knowledge for dense detectors \cite{Zheng2022LD}. Response-based distillation on the detection head, applied to class scores and boxes, is also simple to implement because it needs no extra modules that would remain in the deployed model.

The choice of teacher matters. A larger and more accurate teacher is often not a better one: when the capacity gap between teacher and student is large, the student cannot mimic the teacher \cite{Cho2019Efficacy}, and intermediate-sized teacher assistants have been proposed to bridge the gap \cite{Mirzadeh2020TA}. Distillation also works without a capacity gap. Born-again networks, students with the same architecture as their teacher, outperformed their teachers \cite{Furlanello2018BAN}. One interpretation is that distillation then acts as a regulariser, which would matter most when training data are scarce. D-FINE's localisation self-distillation is a related idea applied inside a single network \cite{Peng2024DFINE}.

Distillation adds no cost at inference and combines naturally with the other methods: it can recover accuracy lost by pruning, and it can be used during quantisation-aware training \cite{Cheng2017SurveyCompression}. Its costs are a teacher, extra training time and additional hyperparameters.

\subsection{Hardware-aware design}
\label{sec:rw_nas}

Neural architecture search (NAS) automates the design of networks by searching a space $\mathcal{A}$ of candidate architectures, often under a cost constraint \cite{Cai2019ProxylessNAS}:
\begin{equation}
\max_{\alpha \in \mathcal{A}} \; \mathrm{Acc}(\alpha)
\quad \text{subject to} \quad
\mathrm{Cost}(\alpha) \le C_{\max},
\label{eq:nas_constraint}
\end{equation}
where the cost can be latency, memory or energy. Hardware-aware methods measure or predict the cost on the target device, because operation counts do not predict latency reliably \cite{Cai2019ProxylessNAS}. Full NAS is expensive, so practical work restricts the search space, searches only parts of a network, or starts from published efficient families \cite{Tan2020EfficientDet}. RF-DETR applies weight-sharing NAS to detection transformers \cite{Robinson2025RFDETR}. Releasing a detector in several scales, as YOLO11 and YOLO26 do, can be seen as a manual version of the same idea: the user picks the scale that fits the budget. NAS is not carried out in this thesis.

\section{Inference on general-purpose processors}
\label{sec:rw_cpu}

Many edge computers have no GPU and run neural networks on their central processor. Processors execute the arithmetic of a network with single-instruction multiple-data (SIMD) vector instructions, such as AVX2 with 256-bit registers or AVX-512 with 512-bit registers on x86 processors. Two further extensions matter for reduced precision. Vector Neural Network Instructions (VNNI) multiply 8-bit integers and accumulate the products into 32-bit sums in a single instruction, which accelerates INT8 convolutions and matrix products. Advanced Matrix Extensions (AMX), introduced with the fourth generation of Intel Xeon Scalable processors, add two-dimensional tile registers and a matrix-multiplication unit for BF16 and INT8 \cite{Nassif2022SapphireRapids}. Server processors may support all of these extensions, whereas low-power and embedded processors often provide only AVX2, possibly with VNNI. Which reduced-precision format is fastest therefore depends on the processor. With AMX, BF16 runs at high throughput without calibration. Without AMX or native BF16 support, INT8 through VNNI is the reduced-precision format that the hardware accelerates.

Inference runtimes translate a trained network into code for the target processor. OpenVINO converts a model into an intermediate representation, applies graph optimisations such as operator fusion, and selects kernel implementations for the instruction set that is available when the model is compiled \cite{OpenVINO}. A precision hint lets the runtime execute an FP32 model in BF16 on processors that support it. An INT8 model produced by quantisation contains quantise and dequantise operations, and layers without an integer kernel run in floating point.

Operation counts are an indirect measure of speed. Latency also depends on memory access, on how well the layer shapes fit the vector and tile units, on data-layout conversions and on parallelism. Ma et al.\ therefore argued for evaluating the direct metric, speed on the target platform, rather than FLOPs alone \cite{Ma2018ShuffleNetV2, Sze2017EfficientProcessing}. Small models in particular can be limited by memory traffic and fixed overheads rather than arithmetic, and the end-to-end time of a detector also includes image resizing and, for most one-stage detectors, NMS. For these reasons, all latencies in this thesis are measured end to end on the processor itself.

\section{Object detection in viticulture}
\label{sec:rw_viticulture}

Deep learning is widely used in agriculture, from crop and weed classification to fruit detection and yield estimation \cite{Kamilaris2018DLAgriculture}. Grape bunches are a difficult detection target. Their shape, size, colour and compactness vary between varieties and growth stages, they are partly hidden by leaves and by each other, and outdoor lighting changes strongly \cite{Santos2020WGISD}.

The Embrapa Wine Grape Instance Segmentation Dataset (WGISD) is a standard benchmark for bunch detection \cite{Santos2020WGISD}. It contains 300 photographs of five grape varieties, taken at close range, with bounding boxes for all bunches and instance masks for a subset of them. Its authors evaluated Mask R-CNN, YOLOv2 and YOLOv3 and combined detection with three-dimensional association to track bunches across images; at IoU 0.5, Mask R-CNN reached an F1 score of 0.84. GrapeMOTS provides drone video recorded along vineyard rows from several perspectives, with multi-object tracking and segmentation annotations of grape bunches \cite{ArizaSentis2024GrapeMOTS}. W{\l}odarczyk et al.\ catalogued public precision-viticulture datasets and assessed their readiness for scene reconstruction \cite{Wlodarczyk2026GrapesReady}. As Chapter~3 shows, only a few of these datasets carry bunch-level labels suitable for detection.

Recent work on WGISD proposes lightweight YOLO variants. GrapeUL-YOLO adds bidirectional cross-scale fusion and elliptical anchors to a YOLO detector \cite{Zhu2026GrapeULYOLO}. On the official test split it reports 91.2 mAP50 and 57.6 mAP50-95 with 5.11 million parameters, ahead of the YOLOv5, YOLOv8 and YOLO11 baselines trained in the same study. Like most such studies, it reports single training runs without uncertainty estimates and measures speed on a desktop GPU. On a test set of 58 images, differences of one or two points can arise from training randomness and test-set sampling alone. The effect of compression and deployment settings on processor latency is rarely studied for this application.

\section{Summary and research gap}
\label{sec:rw_summary}

This chapter introduced the building blocks of modern vision models, the YOLO11 and YOLO26 detector families and the detection transformers that offer an alternative design, together with the main methods for making detectors cheaper: quantisation, structured pruning, knowledge distillation and hardware-aware design. It also explained why the fastest number format and the actual benefit of a smaller network depend on the processor and its instruction set.

Three gaps in the reviewed work motivate this thesis. First, compressed models are usually compared with the model they were derived from, rarely with a smaller dense model of the same family, although the value of pruning over training a smaller architecture has been questioned \cite{Liu2019RethinkingPruning, Blalock2020StatePruning}. Second, savings are often reported as operation counts or as GPU latency, while latency on processors, which depends on the number format and the instruction set, is seldom measured together with accuracy. Third, detection studies on small agricultural datasets report single runs without uncertainty and do not examine deployment on processors. The following chapters address these gaps with a common protocol: the same processor, the same accuracy evaluation with confidence intervals, and a fixed decision rule for when compression counts as better than choosing a smaller model.


\chapter{Experimental methodology}

This chapter defines the experimental plan used to compare model compression strategies for object detection. The central idea is to evaluate a small but representative set of COCO-trained detectors under a shared protocol, then measure how accuracy and runtime change after applying progressively stronger efficiency methods. The study uses COCO because it is a standard object detection benchmark with established evaluation metrics and public checkpoints \cite{Lin2014COCO}.

\section{Research objective and validation logic}

The operational research objective is to determine how different object detector families respond to practical compression methods when they are evaluated under the same COCO-oriented deployment protocol. More precisely, the thesis asks whether transformer-based detectors, hierarchical vision transformers, and YOLO-style convolutional detectors show different accuracy--latency--energy trade-offs after FP16 inference, INT8 post-training quantisation, token reduction, and light structured pruning.

The validation logic has four stages. First, a published COCO checkpoint is evaluated without compression to establish an accuracy and latency baseline. Second, a low-risk precision change (FP16) is measured to check whether the detector is numerically stable under reduced precision. Third, an INT8 deployment path is tested with calibration data and a real inference backend. Fourth, architecture-specific compression is added: token reduction for transformer-style detectors and structured pruning for YOLO-style detectors when the software stack is stable enough.

A compressed model is considered useful only if its accuracy loss is justified by a measurable deployment benefit. Therefore, the methodology does not optimise mAP alone. It compares mAP@0.5:0.95, AP@0.5, median latency, throughput, memory use, and approximate energy per image. The resulting evaluation is intended to identify Pareto-favourable configurations rather than a single universally best detector.

\section{Hardware and practical scope}

The local experimental device is an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3070 Ti with 8 GB of GPU memory. This hardware is powerful enough for inference benchmarking, export experiments, calibration, and short fine-tuning runs, but it is not appropriate for training large COCO detectors from scratch. Therefore, the experimental scope is deliberately focused on:

\begin{itemize}
    \item COCO validation-set evaluation of published checkpoints,
    \item FP32 and FP16 inference on the local GPU,
    \item INT8 post-training quantisation using a representative calibration subset,
    \item token reduction in transformer-style models,
    \item light structured pruning with limited fine-tuning when memory permits.
\end{itemize}

Batch size 1 is the primary latency setting because it corresponds to real-time camera-like inference. Batch size 8 is treated as a secondary throughput setting and may be skipped for the largest variants if memory usage becomes unstable.

\section{Current implementation stage}

The full methodology is broader than the set of measurements already completed. At the current stage, the implemented pipeline covers:

\begin{itemize}
    \item RF-DETR Medium FP32 and FP16 evaluation on the full COCO 2017 validation set,
    \item YOLOv8 and YOLO26 FP32/FP16 inference on the COCO validation split,
    \item YOLOv8 and YOLO26 INT8 post-training quantisation through TensorRT engines,
    \item telemetry collection for latency, throughput, power, energy per image, GPU memory, utilisation, and temperature.
\end{itemize}

The remaining planned work includes Swin-T Mask R-CNN evaluation, RF-DETR INT8 export, transformer token-reduction experiments, and pruning experiments. These components require a more stable Linux-based environment because the OpenMMLab and TensorRT stacks are sensitive to Python, CUDA, PyTorch, and TensorRT versions. For this reason, the results chapter separates completed measurements from planned extensions instead of presenting the whole matrix as finished.

\section{Dataset preparation and calibration protocol}

The evaluation dataset is COCO 2017 validation. The full validation split contains 5000 images, which is used when the model/runtime combination is stable enough for unattended evaluation. For faster iteration, a 500-image validation subset is also used. This subset is not a replacement for the final COCO evaluation, but it is useful for checking whether an export path works, whether labels are mapped correctly, and whether a compression method causes an obvious accuracy collapse.

For YOLO-family models, COCO annotations are converted to the label format expected by the Ultralytics validation pipeline. The conversion preserves COCO category order and writes one text file per image. In addition to the validation subset, a representative calibration subset is prepared for INT8 post-training quantisation. The calibration images are passed through the network during TensorRT export so that activation ranges can be estimated before the final INT8 engine is built. The calibration subset is deliberately drawn from the same data distribution as the validation images, because PTQ quality depends strongly on whether the calibration inputs represent the real inference workload.

\section{Benchmarking workflow}

Each experiment row is represented by a tuple containing the model identifier, precision or compression variant, runtime backend, batch size, dataset split, and image count. The benchmark runner records both the numeric result and the raw metric file for later inspection. A completed row therefore contains at least:

\begin{itemize}
    \item model family and checkpoint identifier,
    \item precision variant (FP32, FP16, INT8 PTQ),
    \item runtime backend (PyTorch, TensorRT, or pending backend),
    \item number of evaluated images,
    \item COCO mAP metrics,
    \item latency, throughput, GPU memory, power, and energy fields when available,
    \item path to the model artifact or exported engine.
\end{itemize}

This row-based design is intentionally simple. It allows partial overnight runs to remain useful even if a later model fails to export or a dependency stack breaks. Failed and skipped rows are kept in the summary file with an explanatory message, which is important for reproducibility because deployment failures are themselves part of the practical evaluation.

\section{Solution architecture and implementation environment}

The experimental implementation is organised as a benchmarking pipeline rather than as a single monolithic script. The pipeline contains five main components:

\begin{itemize}
    \item a data layer that stores COCO images, annotations, converted YOLO labels, and calibration subsets,
    \item model adapters for RF-DETR, YOLO/YOLO26, and the planned MMDetection Swin detector,
    \item an export layer for precision conversion and deployment artifacts such as TensorRT engines and ONNX models,
    \item a benchmark runner that executes each experiment row and records accuracy, latency, and telemetry,
    \item an aggregation layer that writes summary CSV files and presentation/thesis plots.
\end{itemize}

Most implementation code is written in Python because the relevant model and deployment libraries expose Python APIs. The current pipeline uses PyTorch for baseline inference, the Ultralytics package for YOLO-family checkpoints and validation, the RF-DETR package for the transformer-detector baseline, TensorRT for deployment-oriented INT8 engines, pycocotools for COCO metrics and annotation handling, and NVIDIA tooling such as NVML/\texttt{nvidia-smi} for GPU telemetry. PowerShell scripts are used only for local orchestration on Windows; the final full experiment environment is planned for Linux because OpenMMLab, MMCV, MMDetection, MMDeploy, CUDA, and TensorRT are substantially easier to align there.

Existing components are reused wherever possible: published checkpoints, official validation procedures, and standard COCO metrics are preferred over custom reimplementations. The new contribution of the implementation is the unified comparison workflow around those components: consistent experiment rows, calibration handling, telemetry capture, failure logging, and result aggregation across detector families.

\section{Runtime and export backends}

The FP32 and FP16 rows are evaluated with PyTorch unless stated otherwise. FP16 is enabled as a low-risk precision reduction because it does not require calibration and is widely supported on NVIDIA GPUs. However, FP16 speedups are not guaranteed: some models are limited by memory movement, post-processing, Python overhead, or operators that do not benefit much from half precision.

INT8 rows are treated as deployment measurements rather than only numerical-format conversions. For the YOLO-family results, INT8 PTQ uses TensorRT engine export. This matters because TensorRT performs graph optimisation, operator fusion, calibration, and engine generation, so the measured latency reflects the deployment runtime rather than raw PyTorch execution. The comparison between FP32/FP16 PyTorch and INT8 TensorRT is therefore a practical deployment comparison, not a pure isolated precision comparison.

For RF-DETR and Swin, the intended final path is similar in spirit but more involved. RF-DETR requires a reliable ONNX/TensorRT export path for the transformer detector and its post-processing. Swin-T Mask R-CNN is planned through the OpenMMLab/MMDetection ecosystem, with deployment through MMDeploy or ONNX/TensorRT where feasible. These paths are postponed to the final environment because earlier Windows and Colab attempts showed sensitivity to CUDA, PyTorch, MMCV, and TensorRT versions.

\section{Telemetry collection}

Accuracy alone is insufficient for this thesis because a detector with strong mAP can still be impractical if it is slow or energy-intensive. Therefore, the benchmark records deployment-oriented quantities alongside COCO metrics. Median latency is the main timing metric for batch-size-1 inference. Throughput is computed from latency when appropriate. GPU power, utilisation, temperature, and memory are sampled during the run using NVIDIA tooling where available. Energy per image is estimated from the observed power and throughput window.

These telemetry values should be interpreted as approximate but useful. They depend on driver version, GPU clocks, cooling, background processes, and runtime implementation. Nevertheless, they reveal effects that FLOPs alone cannot capture, such as the difference between a PyTorch model and an optimised TensorRT engine.

\section{Compression methods}

The compression study varies three main dimensions.

\subsection{Token reduction}

Token reduction applies only to transformer-style detectors. The baseline implementation will use a training-free token merging strategy inspired by Token Merging (ToMe), MaMe, and Mutual Pair Merging (MPM) \cite{Bolya2023ToMe, MaMe2026, MPM2026}. In RF-DETR, token merging will be inserted in encoder layers before the decoder consumes image features. In Swin, token reduction will be explored either within local windows or after window attention, depending on which implementation causes the smallest disruption to the MMDetection model.

The initial token-reduction setting should be conservative: one mid-network merge point and a small merge ratio. More aggressive settings are added only after the baseline setting has been validated on a COCO subset.

\subsection{Quantisation}

The quantisation comparison includes FP32, FP16, and INT8 post-training quantisation. FP16 measures the benefit of using tensor-core-friendly reduced precision. INT8 PTQ requires calibration on a representative COCO subset before evaluating on the validation set. Quantisation-aware training is reserved as an optional extension for one model family if PTQ produces a large accuracy loss.

In the implemented YOLO pipeline, INT8 PTQ is performed by exporting TensorRT engines calibrated on COCO validation images. This is the main deployment-oriented INT8 path because it measures the runtime that would actually be used for accelerated inference. ONNX Runtime INT8 quantisation was also prepared as a fallback for producing quantised ONNX models, but it is treated separately from TensorRT because CPU ONNX Runtime latency is not directly comparable with GPU TensorRT latency.

\subsection{Structured pruning}

Structured pruning removes complete channels, filters, attention heads, or blocks, rather than individual unstructured weights. The main reason is practical: structured changes are more likely to reduce real latency on common inference backends \cite{Li2017PruningFilters}. In this thesis, pruning is treated as a light final compression step after the more deployment-friendly methods have been tested.

\section{Evaluation metrics}

Each model and compression variant will be evaluated using the following metrics:

\begin{itemize}
    \item COCO mAP@[0.5:0.95] as the primary accuracy metric,
    \item AP@0.5 as a more permissive detection-quality metric,
    \item latency in milliseconds for batch size 1 and, where feasible, batch size 8,
    \item parameters and model file size,
    \item FLOPs or MACs, reported as a proxy metric rather than a replacement for measured latency,
    \item peak GPU memory during inference,
    \item optional power draw or energy per image from repeated benchmark windows.
\end{itemize}

Latency measurements will use warm-up iterations followed by repeated timed iterations with CUDA synchronisation. Results will be reported as median latency and, where possible, an interquartile range. This avoids over-interpreting single measurements affected by background processes or GPU clock changes.

\section{Robustness extension}

If the main matrix is completed early, a robustness extension can evaluate a subset of compressed detectors under controlled input degradations: Gaussian noise, blur, and JPEG compression. This extension is useful because quantisation and token reduction may change not only average accuracy, but also how quickly a detector degrades when image quality becomes worse.

\section{Threats to validity}

Several limitations must be documented. First, RF-DETR, Swin Mask R-CNN, and YOLO differ not only in backbone design, but also in detection heads, training recipes, input resolutions, and post-processing. Second, latency depends strongly on the inference runtime, exported graph, driver version, and operator support. Third, an RTX 3070 Ti is a useful local deployment proxy, but it is not the same as an embedded edge accelerator. For these reasons, conclusions should emphasise measured trade-offs under the stated protocol rather than universal claims about every deployment setting.
\chapter{Preliminary results}

This chapter reports the measurements completed so far. The results are preliminary: they validate the benchmarking and export workflow, but they do not yet cover the full final matrix described in Chapter~3. The reported RF-DETR, YOLOv8, and YOLO26 accuracy values are presented as COCO 2017 validation measurements on the full 5000-image validation split. A separate 500-image subset is used for INT8 calibration and rapid smoke tests, but it is not the basis of the reported mAP values in the main result tables.

\section{Completed experiment scope}

The completed measurements cover two parts of the planned comparison. First, RF-DETR Medium was evaluated in FP32 and FP16 precision using the PyTorch runtime on the full COCO validation set. Second, YOLOv8s, YOLOv8m, YOLO26s, and YOLO26m were evaluated in FP32, FP16, and INT8 TensorRT precision with the same COCO validation target. The Swin-T Mask R-CNN part of the matrix and transformer-specific token-reduction experiments remain planned work.

The reported latency is median per-image latency at batch size 1. For the YOLO INT8 rows, the runtime is TensorRT and the engines were produced through post-training quantisation with a COCO calibration subset. The raw result artifacts are retained under the project \texttt{runs/} directory together with exported model artifacts and plot files used in the presentation.

\section{Data used in the preliminary experiments}

The preliminary experiments use the COCO 2017 validation split. The full validation set contains 5000 images and an annotation file in JSON format. Each image entry contains fields such as image identifier, file name, width, and height. Each object annotation contains an image identifier, category identifier, bounding box, segmentation metadata, object area, and an \texttt{iscrowd} flag. The most important numeric fields for this thesis are the bounding boxes and category identifiers, because they are used to compute COCO mAP and AP@0.5.

For YOLO-family validation, the COCO annotations are transformed into the Ultralytics label format. In this format, each image has a corresponding text file containing class identifiers and normalised bounding boxes. The transformation changes the representation of the labels but not the evaluation target: the model is still evaluated against COCO object categories. The benchmark also creates a smaller calibration subset for INT8 PTQ. Calibration images are used only to estimate activation ranges during export; they are not treated as training data and are not used to report final accuracy.

No detector is trained from scratch in the preliminary stage. The models use published COCO-trained checkpoints, so the COCO training split is part of the original checkpoint training process rather than part of the local experiment. Locally, the validation split is used in two ways: the full 5000-image set for the reported accuracy measurements, and a 500-image subset for calibration or rapid export checks. This distinction matters because the calibration subset is an implementation input for INT8 PTQ, while the full validation split is the benchmark target for reported mAP.

The telemetry collected during the same runs also indicates that runtime choice can strongly affect energy per image. \section{Implications for the next experimental stage}

The preliminary results motivate three concrete next steps. First, YOLO-family experiments should be repeated in the final Linux environment to confirm that the observed INT8 accuracy drops and FP16 latency behaviour are stable across runs and software stacks. Second, RF-DETR should be exported to an optimised backend before conclusions are drawn about transformer detector latency. The PyTorch RF-DETR numbers are useful as a baseline, but they do not yet represent the best deployment path. Third, Swin-T Mask R-CNN should be added once the OpenMMLab stack is stable, because it is the missing hierarchical transformer point needed to complete the architectural comparison.

The results also refine the compression plan. FP16 can be treated as a low-risk baseline because it preserves accuracy across the completed experiments. INT8 PTQ is more powerful but model-dependent: it provides large latency and energy gains, but the mAP loss can vary substantially. Token reduction and pruning should therefore be evaluated after stable FP32/FP16/INT8 baselines are available, otherwise it becomes difficult to attribute accuracy loss to a particular compression step.

\section{Error sources and uncertainty}

The main source of statistical uncertainty is not the validation target itself, but the small number of repeated timing runs and the evolving software environment. COCO mAP is reported against the validation target, while the 500-image subset is used for calibration and smoke testing. The full thesis evaluation should still repeat the YOLO and YOLO26 measurements in the final Linux environment and, where possible, report latency variation across repeated runs.

There is also implementation uncertainty. The FP32 and FP16 results use PyTorch, while the INT8 results use TensorRT. This is appropriate for deployment evaluation, but it means that speedups reflect both lower numerical precision and backend optimisation. TensorRT version changes can also affect export success, layer fusion, calibration behaviour, and latency. This was already visible in the development process, where some INT8 export paths worked in one Colab/runtime configuration but failed in another.

Energy measurements are approximate because they are derived from sampled GPU telemetry rather than from an external power meter. They are useful for comparing broad runtime trends, especially when the same machine and protocol are used, but they should not be interpreted as exact physical measurements. Finally, Swin and token-reduction results are not yet present, so architectural conclusions about hierarchical ViTs and token compression remain hypotheses until the final experiment matrix is completed.

\section{Current limitations}

The current results should be interpreted cautiously. Although the reported accuracy values are aligned to the COCO validation target, the FP16 and INT8 rows use different runtimes: FP16 measurements were PyTorch-based, whereas INT8 measurements used TensorRT engines. As a result, part of the observed speedup comes from the deployment runtime and graph optimisation, not only from lower numerical precision. The YOLO26s FP16 regression also shows that runtime-specific kernel support can dominate the expected benefit of reduced precision. Finally, Swin Transformer results are not yet included because the OpenMMLab stack was unstable in the available Windows/Colab environments. A Linux installation is planned for the final version of the experiments.
